Summary of KVG validation progress

Alex Pate

2026-06-08

This document contains simulations to ensure methods to assess calibration of the CHARIOT model using the artificial censoring approach detailed in “Prediction Under Interventions: Evaluation of Counterfactual Performance Using Longitudinal Observational Data (Keogh and Van Geloven)” are being applied appropriately.

In our real data analyses, we do not report weighted Kaplan-Meier vs Mean Predicted risk, or a similar analysis within groups defined by deciles of predicted risk, as is done in the K&VG paper. There are a few reasons for this.

Firstly, our data is continuously observed, and 10-year risk scores contain 3652 different possible event times. Given the size of the cohort, the majority of these event times are observed. A weighted Kaplan-Meier requires time-varying weights at every possible event time, which is not feasible in this data. One option would be to bin the data at 30/90/180 intervals, and apply weighted Kaplan-Meier estimator to this data. However, the impact of this choice, and magnitude of the bias induced by doing so, is unclear. There are alternative approaches (detailed in this document) which do not suffer this issue.

Secondly, our censoring mechanism is not independent from the outcome mechanism. Primarily due to individuals at high risk of death (censoring event), also being more likely to experience the event. Assessment of mean calibration through Kaplan-Meier observed risk vs mean predicted risk will be biased, as the Kaplan-Meier estimate will be biased. This is protected somewhat by looking at weighted Kaplan-Meier vs Mean Predicted risk within groups of individuals with similar risk (i.e. deciles), however we prefer to address this more directly by using IPCW weights, and calibration estimators of a different form.

The methods we choose to use are pseudo-values, and cross sectional approaches looking at who has and hasn’t had the event by the time point of interest. Both must be weighted using IPCW’s and IPACW’s. We demonstrate approaches to estimate mean calibration, and produce smoothed moderate calibration curves. Given these approaches are not used in the K&VG paper, I wanted to run simulations to make sure they do indeed recover the correct calibration curve.

A final note, in these simulations, we assume all confounding between the outcome, and the censoring mechanisms, can be explained by variables measured at baseline. This means time-varying confounding can be ignored when estimating the weights, however, in practice, the weights could be estimated in a way that accounts for time varying confounding, like is done in K&VG. For example, pooled logistic regression could be used. In these simulations, we actually use the true values of the weights (known from the data generating mechanism) rather than estimating them, so this is effectively a moot point. The goal is to show how we can accurately assess mean calibration, and moderate calibration, using weights derived from two separate censoring mechanisms, avoiding the Kaplan-Meier approach. The two censoring mechanisms reflect the normal censoring, and artificial censoring from the K&VG paper.

1 Simulation - one censoring mechanism

The first simulation just uses one censoring mechanism. The goal is to showcase how to assess mean calibration of a model predicting a survival outcome, using a range of IPCW approaches, where there is dependent censoring between the outcome and censoring mechanism in the validation data. We also show that using standard IPCW weights (not time-varying weights) in conjunction with Kaplan-Meier estimator will be biased.

Note that these methods can be extended to produce moderate calibration curves. We have shown this in on-going work (currently under review with D&PR). These extensions are implemented in the second simulation, which involves two censoring mechanisms.

1.1 Simulation setup

Simulate survival times and censoring times. Both have an exponential hazard, and share a single predictor with a log-hazard of 1, proportional hazard.

1.2 Mean calibration

We know the prediction model is perfectly specified and risks are correct. Mean calibration should therefore indicate perfect calibration.

Mean predicted risk vs Kaplan-Meier observed risk indicates bias. This is expected given it assumes random censoring:

## [1] 0.3888313

## [1] 1.237206

Next we use a Horvitz-Thompson Estimator. An outcome indicator is defined for who has (\(Y_{eval} = 1\)) and hasn’t (\(Y_{eval} = 0\)) had the event by \(t_{eval}\). The indicator itself is multiplied by the weights, and then we take the mean. We compare this with the mean predicted risk, and find this indicates perfect mean calibration:

### Horvitz-Thompson estimator
ht_estimate <- mean(
  (df_valid$cvd_indicator == 1 & df_valid$cvd_time <= t_fup) * 
    (df_valid$ipcw_weight_true_cap)
)
# obs
ht_estimate
## [1] 0.3896541
# pred
mean(1 - df_valid$cvd_surv)
## [1] 0.3888313
# ratio
mean(1 - df_valid$cvd_surv)/ht_estimate
## [1] 0.9978885

Next we use a weighted mean estimator in the subset of uncensored individuals at 10-years. This is a Hajek style estimator. This includes those who have had the event prior to 10-years, or who have not had the event and are uncensored at 10-years. Rather than multiplying the outcome indicator \(Y_{eval}\) by the weight and taking the mean across the whole validation cohort (Horvitz-Thompson), we take a weighted mean in the subset of uncensored individuals.

The difference between the Hajek style and Horvitz-Thompson estimator, is that the denominator when calculating the mean for the Hajek style estimator is 1/sum(weights), rather than 1/n. More generally, the Hajek style estimator is a stabilised version of the Horvitz-Thompson estimator. However, in this setting, one would expect the Horvitz-Thompson estimator to have smaller variance, given it’s estimated on the entire dataset, rather than just the uncensored individuals. I need to look into this in more detail, benefits of stabilisation vs greater variance of the estimator, and whether this even holds.

### Weighted mean estimator (Hajek-style: weights in numerator and denominator)
weighted_subset_estimate <- weighted.mean(df_valid_uncensored$Y,

The issue with the Kaplan-Meier approach, is that we need to have time-varying weights, that are defined at every step/break-point in the Kaplan-Meier estimator. There is also possibly some conflation here, because Kaplan-Meier deals with censoring in it’s own way, but it assumes that the censoring is completely random.

Next, I am going to repeat the experiment, but with two censoring mechanisms, to more accurately replicate what’s done in the KVG paper, and also when implementing this method in practice.

2 Simulation - two censoring mechanisms (one random, one dependent)

This simulation now applies two censoring mechanisms. The first reflects the regular censoring mechanisms, and the second represents the artificial censoring mechanism applied in the K&VG work. Only the artificial censoring mechanism is dependent with the outcome. This is to ensure that when applying non time-varying weights (for the IPACWs) to the Kaplan-Meier estimator, we have no bias induced by the regular censoring mechanism. In section 3, we have a simulation where both the censoring mechanisms are dependent.

2.1 Simulation setup

Simulate survival time, censoring times, and artificial censoring times. All have exponential hazard.

The censoring time is completely random. The artificial censoring time and survival time share a single predictor (x) with a log-hazard of 1, proportional hazards. The censoring time is chosen to be completely random, so that we know the Kaplan-Meier estimator is not biased through this.

The weights are now calculating by fitting two models. IPC weights are estimated by fitting a model in the not artificially censored cohort, to predict the risk of being censored. The IPAC weights are estimated by fitting a model in the artificially censored cohort, to predict the risk of being artificially censored. Both weights are calculated by evaluating the probability of being uncensored at \(min\{t_{eval}, t_{event}, t_{censored}, t_{artif censored}\}\). The combined weights are defined as IPCW*IPACW, as proposed in KVG work.

Note, it may feel natural to estimate the IPC weights at \(min{t_{eval}, t_{event}, t_{censored}}\), which is the weight that would be estimated in a cohort without artificial censoring. I have tried this and this does not impact results. This minimum of these only changes for individuals who are artificially censored, and these individuals will always contribute zero to the outcome model, and \(0*weight = 0\). Therefore either is fine.

2.2 Mean calibration

We start by using the Kaplan-Meier estimator and weighting this with the IPACW weights. Note, this is implemented incorrectly. These should be time-varying weights. We therefore expect a biased assessment of calibration, and indeed, this indicates miscalibration, when we know the model to be perfectly calibrated. Estimating time-varying weights for this would be very computationally heavy.

### Using Kaplan-Meier without adjustment for informative censoring results in bias
km_fit_weighted <- survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ 1, 
                           data = df_valid_ipacw[1:100000, ], 
                           weights = df_valid_ipacw[1:100000, "ipacw_weight_cap"])
# obs
1 - summary(km_fit_weighted, times = round(10*365.25))$surv
## [1] 0.3182638
# pred
mean(1 - df_valid_ipacw$cvd_surv)
## [1] 0.3885607
# ratio
mean(1 - df_valid_ipacw$cvd_surv)/1 - summary(km_fit_weighted, times = round(10*365.25))$surv
## [1] -0.2931755

Appropriate calibration using Horvitz-Thompson Estimator:

### Horvitz-Thompson
ht_estimate <- mean(
  (df_valid_ipacw$status == 1 & df_valid_ipacw$time <= t_fup) * 
    (df_valid_ipacw$comb_weights_cap)
)
# obs
ht_estimate
## [1] 0.3889177
# pred
mean(1 - df_valid_ipacw$cvd_surv)
## [1] 0.3885607
# ratio
mean(1 - df_valid_ipacw$cvd_surv)/ht_estimate
## [1] 0.9990821

### Weighted mean estimator (Hajek-style: weights in numerator and denominator)

# obs

## [1] 0.3890239

## [1] 0.3885607
# ratio
mean(1 - df_valid_ipacw$cvd_surv)/weighted_subset_estimate
## [1] 0.9988093

So, we have identified that time-varying weights are needed for the Kaplan-Meier. This is indeed what is done in KVG approach, and the Kaplan-Meier is fitted to weighted interval censored data. The difference there is that they have data with visits at fixed integer times. For us, having 1-day intervals over 10-years, and weights at every interval would be computationally very difficult.

The solution would be to discretise our data into bigger intervals, say 180 days or 1-year. However, the impact and bias induced by this is unclear. We have also shown it isn’t necessary, because we can estimate observed risk using other approaches which are less computationally demanding.

Another approach we haven’t yet discussed is the use of pseudo-values. Weighted pseudo-values can be used to assess calibration. See Overgaard et al; DOI: 10.1016/j.jspi.2019.02.003 and Binder et al; DOI: 10.1007/s10985-013-9247-7. This is again reasonably computationally demanding, and so for the purpose of these simulations, we have not implemented for the analyses looking at mean calibration. We do include this approach in the following section, where we consider moderate calibration curves. To estimate mean calibration, simply compare mean predicted risk with the mean of the pseudo-values.

2.3 Moderate calibration

From our other work (REF XXXX), we have developed methods that can produce calibration plots in the presence of dependent censoring.

For the first approach, we look in the cohort of individuals uncensored at time \(t_{eval}\), and apply methods for producing calibration curves for a binary logistic regression model, with the addition of IPC weights (BLR-IPCW). The second approach uses weighted pseudo-values. Both of these methods work in this setting too:

As a result of this work, we have also extended the Horvitz-Thompson estimator approach for mean calibration to produce calibration curves. We multiply the outcome indicator (\(Y_{eval}\)) by the weights, and then apply calibration methods that would be used for a linear model (regression weighted outcome on predictions and then estimate fitted values as a function of the predictions). This is done within the entire validation cohort (not just those that are uncensored at \(t_{eval}\)). We refer to this as smoothed HT approach.

We report the calibration plot and integrated calibration index (mean magnitude of bias of the calibration curve) for each method.

Based on our ongoing work (REF XXXX), I think this is because the prediction model is perfectly specified, and therefore the censoring mechanism (combined normal and artificial) is conditionally independent from the outcome in the validation dataset, given the predicted risks. 4 A note on time-varying confounding

We have ignored time-varying confounding in these simulations. The goal of these simulations was to show how to assess calibration (mean or moderate) using approaches avoiding the Kaplan-Meier estimator. We assumed that we can accurately estimate or know the weights. If there is time-dependent confounding between treatment initiation (artificial censoring) and the outcome, this would need to be reflected in the process for estimating the weights. This could either be a pooled logistic regression model, or an interval censored survival model. However, this does induce extra considerations, as the IPC and IPAC weights are estimated at \(min\{t_{eval}, t_{event}, t_{censored}, t_{artif censored}\}\).

Currently, our models for estimating the weights (cox proportional hazard models) allow us to estimate probabilities of censoring at these exact times. If we use a pooled logistic regression models in order to estimate the weights, then we will lose the ability to do this. This is based on the assumption that the pooled logistic regression model would be implemented within intervals of say 30/90/180/365 days. While a pooled logistic regression model could be implemented with 1 day intervals, again this introduces a large computational burden, and potentially insufficient sample sizes/event rates for the individual logistic regression models.

An alternative is to use interval censored survival models to estimate the weights. This would allow us to both adjustment for time-varying confounding (at say 1 year intervals), and still estimate weights at the exact times of \(min\{t_{eval}, t_{event}, t_{censored}, t_{artif censored}\}\). Once estimated, these weights (estimated with adjustment for time-varying confounding), can then be used in the same way as the weights have been throughout this manuscript, in conjunction with the Hajek style/Horvitz Thompson estimators of mean calibration, and the BLR-IPCW, smoothed-HT and weighted pseudo-values approaches for estimating calibration curves. We prefer these approaches over the Kaplan-Meier approach for computational reasons, and more explicit handling of the non-artificial censoring mechanism.

5 A small note

The other goal of the simulation was to establish whether the IPC weights should be evaluated at \(min\{t_{eval}, t_{event}, t_{censored}, t_{artif censored}\}\) (event/censoring time observed in artificially censored data) or \(min\{t_{eval}, t_{event}, t_{censored}\}\) (event/censoring time observed in normal data). We found that this had no impact, because this only changes for individuals who are artificially censored, and these individuals will always contribute zero to the outcome model, and \(0*weight = 0\), under the approaches we are implemented. Therefore either is fine. There may be situations where this doesn’t hold.
